Planting Delta Bombs

What the "Delta Bomb" actually is, why a debit spread can be entered for a credit, the conditions that have to exist, how the 50Δ/20Δ anchor turns into three bombs, why the call side is harder — and a Charlie McElligott read on the whole thing.

Sources: Delta Bombs White Paper (PDF) · PMTraders "Pandar Tail Hedging Master Class" thread, 465 msgs, Dec 18 2024 → Jan 7 2026 Compiled 2026-08-16 · v2 with real-tape replay
The premise in six lines
  1. A Delta Bomb is a vertical put debit spread (long the higher strike, short the lower strike, same expiry) that ends up in your book for a net credit because you legged in: sold the lower strike when puts were rich (dip / vol pop) and bought the higher strike when puts were cheap (bounce / vol crush).
  2. It works because a 5-pt SPX put spread at ~15–20Δ is only worth ~$0.75, while each leg moves ~$1.35 per 5 SPX points and ~$5 per vol point. A modest intraday round-trip between the two fills is enough to flip a $0.75 debit into a credit. Negative spot–vol correlation (dip → IV up, bounce → IV down) helps both legs on the put side.
  3. Once planted, the spread has zero further downside, uses no buying power (at most brokers), and pays $5 × 100 per contract if SPX settles below the short strike. Hold as a hedge, or close as a unit when it inflates (median 1.45, GTC at ~2.90 on a 5-wide, per the paper's 57-trade sample).
  4. The only real risk is the leg: between fills you are naked long or naked short one put. 95% plant (median 7 min); the 5% that "get away" are the cost — average 60 min hold before cutting. The 50/20 anchor and the "synthetic spread" cover exist to cap that. Replayed on real SPXW quotes (§4, step-by-step on an ordinary Wednesday: 2024-01-17): the anchor rule plants ~1 bomb on an ordinary day, 3+ only on event whipsaws (FOMC 2025-09-17: three in 75 min), and the anchor's own P&L — not the credits — decides the day.
  5. Two lineages: the white paper (SPX, 20–40 DTE, 5-wide, ~1σ OTM, intraday legging, resting orders) and PandarBear's (NVDA/SPY/SPX, deep-OTM $5-wide spreads for ≤$0.10, legged over days–weeks off tail-vol expansion/compression, funded by outperformance vs SPX). Same idea, different clock speeds.
  6. Calls mirror the structure but fight the spot–vol correlation on both legs, so they plant less often; they work in "spot-up/vol-up" tapes (call skew bid — squeezes, AI names, earnings), and Pandar's right-tail use is different again: buy far-OTM call spreads on puke days, sell them into the V.

2 · Why a debit spread can be bought for a credit

A put debit spread (long Khi, short Klo) is normally a debit because the higher-strike put is worth more. You get a credit only by separating the two fills in time and letting the market hand you a better price on each: sell Klo when it's rich, buy Khi when it's cheap. Three things make that realistic in the SPX wings:

  • The spread is thin. Black-Scholes, SPX 5450, 30 DTE, ATM IV 14% with normal put skew: the 5450P is ~$78.5 (46Δ), the 5250P ~$29.4 (20Δ), the 5255P ~$30.2. The 5255/5250 spread is worth ~$0.73. That's the whole hurdle, plus bid/ask (~$0.20–0.50 per leg on SPX at that delta).
  • The legs are fat. The 20Δ put moves ~$2.7 per 10 SPX points from delta alone, and ~$5.3 per vol point. SPX wobbles 10–15 points many times a day; wing IV wobbles 0.3–0.5 vol on the same wobble.
  • Spot–vol correlation stacks the deck on the put side. The dip that makes you sell Klo also lifts IV; the bounce that lets you buy Khi also crushes IV. Vanna works for you on both legs. (On calls it works against you on both legs — see §6.)

Idealised: real fills catch a fraction of the swing and pay the spread. The paper's own hedging model (Fig 3) assumes $0.10 credit per bomb, which is a truer picture of the per-bomb edge than the table above. The edge is not the credit — it's that you now own convexity you didn't pay for.

What you actually own after planting

  • Max value at expiry: width × 100 ($500 on a 5-wide) if SPX ≤ Klo; zero if SPX ≥ Khi. Plus the credit you kept.
  • Buying power: a long vertical held for a credit needs no margin at most brokers ("restored buying power"). Pandar's caveat: IBKR and Schwab both increased margin on him once the inventory of long verticals got large (concentration/pin logic).
  • Probability of Touch ≈ 2 × delta: a 20Δ short strike is touched ~40% of the time before expiry — that's the alpha-mode thesis. The paper's 57 closed bombs: median exit 1.45, upper 2.90, outliers 3.90 on a 5-wide.

3 · SPX put bombs — conditions and playbook put side

Conditions that need to exist

Tape / regime

  • Two-way, mean-reverting intraday movement. The trade is dead in a one-directional day (either direction). Best in a pinned / dealer long-gamma tape.
  • Not already sold off. Pandar: "the longer and higher the market goes up, the better the risk/reward"; after a big drop, IV is elevated and downside is "used up" — he stops buying and instead sells the ITM winners and rolls to lower strikes.
  • Buy legs into complacency, sell legs into panic. "Have to buy during calmer times when the market is complacent and seemingly underpricing risk." Sell tails when they're "expanded": underlying up, IV down, and the put still pricier than two days ago = mispriced tail.

Contract

  • DTE 20–40 (paper) — enough time for the touch, fast enough decay on the short leg. Pandar: current + next monthly.
  • Strike ~1σ OTM (≈16Δ at 30 DTE) for the intraday version; short 30Δ / long 10Δ is the paper's PoT example. For a doomsday hedge go where a black swan actually lands (–15/–30%).
  • Width 5 pts on SPX for speed of fill; wider only when the chain is flat/cheap.
  • SPX over SPY: cash settlement (no DNE calls at 5pm, no pin/assignment), 1256 tax, tiny commission per notional.

Two ways to plant

A · Short leg first (bullish/neutral tape; the community's "reliable" version)

  1. On a dip / vol pop, STO the Klo put (~15–20Δ, or deep OTM Pandar-style) at a size you could carry naked or be assigned on.
  2. Immediately rest a GTC BTO on Khi = Klo+5 at (short fill – target credit). nybhh: "$1 more than the long was bought for", i.e. $1 better than the other leg.
  3. Bounce / IV crush fills the long → bomb planted, credit kept.
  4. If it doesn't bounce: you're short a put — Pandar's rule is to size so that's fine and treat it as normal tail selling with "aggressive profit taking to turn into a spread".

B · Long leg first (bearish tape; hedgers who need protection on immediately)

  1. On a bounce / calm, BTO the Khi put.
  2. Rest a GTC STO on Klo at (long fill + target credit).
  3. The next dip fills the short. Long portfolio offsets the interim mark on the long put ("initiate long legs when hedging a long portfolio").
  4. If it rallies away and won't fill: cut it or accept it as the "cost of maintaining the hedge" (Pandar's words). Median failed-plant hold was 60 min — put a clock on it.

Exits

  • Hedge mode (paper's preference): hold to expiration; the book of bombs is a free put spread ladder against your delta.
  • Alpha mode: exit as a unit (never leg out — you'd re-open the leg risk); standing GTC close ~2.90 on a 5-wide; median 1.45. Pandar sells into any expansion once inventory is large, and rolls near-DTE spreads to further DTE for the smallest loss.
  • ITM winners: take profit and redeploy into lower strikes for the next leg down ("sold the April mopex ones, bought May/June 400–475").

Risk rules that appear in both sources

  • Narrow width → faster fills → less time naked.
  • Time stop on unplanted legs (paper: failed plants held ~60 min avg — that's the tell).
  • Synthetic-spread cover (§2.1c): if only your long is on and the market runs away, sell a further-OTM put now to cap it; you now hold a wide debit spread you can later split into two bombs.
  • 50/20 anchor (§2.1b): hold an ATM long put while you plant, so any short you add is always covered. Walkthrough next.
  • Sizing: Pandar's "conservative" for naked tails = lose ≤10% NLV if the underlying goes to zero; hedge budget = a slice (he says ~50%) of gains above the index; if core holdings are levered, "the best first hedge is deleveraging".

4 · How a 50/20 turns into three bombs — on real tape

The white paper (§2.1b) says: buy a 50Δ/20Δ put spread as a protected anchor, use the 20Δ short "as a base to plant additional Delta Bombs", end the day with one 50Δ put and several 20Δ debit spreads, then close the 50Δ put. It never spells out the order sequence. The sequence that keeps you defined-risk the whole time is: sell the base strike on dips, buy the strike above it on bounces, one pair at a time; the ATM long covers whichever short is momentarily unpaired. Below is that rule replayed on real SPXW quotes.

The whole idea in one picture

Replay setup (mechanical, conservative — scripts/replay_50_20.py)

  • Data: ThetaData 5-minute bid/ask/delta/IV for every SPXW put in the ~30-DTE expiry, 09:30–15:50 ET (central_trade_data/thetadata/lrrf_spxw_1550_5m…/raw/greeks); SPX 1-min index bars for the chart.
  • t0 = 09:35: BTO the ~50Δ put at the ask, STO the ~20Δ put (K) at the bid.
  • Then alternate resting limits: short K open at price s → rest BTO K+5 @ s − 0.50, fills when the 5-min ask ≤ limit; long K+5 filled at b → rest STO K @ b + 0.50, fills when the 5-min bid ≥ limit. Fill = the limit price (no price improvement credited even when the market gaps through it).
  • 15:50: STC the anchor at the bid. One unpaired short max, always covered by the anchor.

A "bomb" today = long 4580P / short 4575P. At expiry it's worth 5.00 if SPX < 4575, zero if SPX > 4580. Right now the market values it at ~0.70 (mid-to-mid); if you just bought it you'd pay ~1.00 (25.40 – 24.40, crossing both spreads). Goal: own it for a small credit — sell the 4575P at some price X, later buy the 4580P at X – 0.25. Cost-neutral would be "at X". Everything after the first two fills is a resting GTC-day limit order; you're not watching the screen, the order is.

Rule of thumb for "how far does SPX have to move": a 20Δ put moves ~0.20 per SPX point, and IV on the wing drifts ~0.2–0.4 vol with a 10-pt move (worth another ~0.5–1.0). To turn a 24.40 sale into a ≤24.15 purchase one strike higher, the 4580P has to fall 1.25 from its 25.40 offer → ~5–7 SPX points up from where you sold. Same distance back down to re-sell. That's the whole game: 5–7-pt wiggles, one strike apart, one pair at a time.

Mean –1.35/day — an ATM put bleeds theta and the index drifts up. It is a hedge itself: it made +107 on 2024-12-18 (hawkish FOMC, SPX –142) and +50 on 2025-02-27 (–106), and lost 41 on 2025-02-28 (+74).

What the real replay changes about the story. (1) The +0.50-per-pair credit is real and repeatable — the two legs swing $4–14 while the 5-wide is worth $0.60–0.90, so the fills happen. (2) Three bombs in a day needs a specific tape: an event whipsaw with 10–15-pt swings every 20–40 minutes after your first short is on (FOMC, CPI, Powell). On an ordinary day the honest number is one, sometimes zero. (3) The anchor is not a footnote — its day P&L (±3 typical, ±20–100 on event days) is an order of magnitude bigger than the credits, so the 50/20 version only makes sense if you want to be long an ATM put (i.e. it's your hedge and you'd hold it anyway) or you take the anchor off as soon as planting is done. (4) Which leg you do first is a coin flip at 09:35 — short-first lost the morning on 11/20 and 9/17; long-first would have planted immediately on both. The anchor is what makes being wrong about that survivable.

Caveats: 5-minute snapshots understate fills (intrabar touches invisible) and overstate them slightly (limit assumed to fill on any bar touching it); fills booked at the limit, not the through-price; anchor/base at ask/bid (crossing) — mid would improve every number by ~0.15–0.25 per leg. Bombing the 50

  • As its own ladder it plants at least as often as the 20Δ (1.10/day vs 1.06) — an ATM put moves ~0.50 per SPX point, so a 6–8-pt wiggle is enough: on quiet 2024-03-13 the 5200/5195 ladder planted two ATM bombs off 6-pt dips at 10:05 and 15:30 while the 20Δ anchor version planted none. Each ATM bomb marks ~2.0–2.5 (max 5) instead of ~0.75, so the "+0.50 credit" is a smaller fraction of what you get — but the thing you get is a 1%-OTM 5-wide, i.e. first-loss protection, not a tail hedge. Its day P&L is wider (–4.0 mean) because a leftover ATM long is a bigger position on rally days.
  • Bombing the anchor itself (turn the 50/20 anchor's long into a 5-wide for a credit on a dip) is the best thing that can happen to the day: on all 13 days it fired, day P&L was positive (+3.25 to +7.35). On CPI-day 2024-04-10 it converts the –5.90 result into +7.35: four 20Δ bombs, then at 15:00 (SPX 5144, 7 pts under the 09:35 level) STO 5170P @ 83.10 against the 5175P bought at 82.60 → the anchor is now a 5175/5170 spread carried at +0.50, marked 2.05 at the close, and its –8.40 bleed is gone. Two constraints: it only fires when SPX is ~6+ pts below where you bought the anchor while your 20Δ short is paired (14% of days from a 09:35 anchor), and once the anchor is a 5-wide it no longer backstops a naked 20Δ short — so it's the last move of the day, not the first.

Why serial planting tops out at ~1 and what gets you 2–3 on an ordinary day

The 50/20 sequence is serial: bomb #2 can't start until bomb #1's long has filled, so three bombs need three full dip→bounce cycles of ~10–15 pts in a row, after your first short is on. That is an FOMC/CPI tape. On an ordinary day SPX gives you one or two such cycles, often late. The fix is parallel: put the first legs of several bombs on at once at different strikes with resting second legs, so one dip (or one bounce) plants all of them together — which is exactly the paper's "rapid planting of multiple bombs" and Pandar's "sell puts then set resting buy orders at different strikes".

What the parallel ladder costs

On the ~30% of days with no 8–10-pt dip after 09:35, you go home long three puts you paid the ask for. That's the entire left tail of the long-first family (worst –61 for three units, 2025-03-31, +105 rally). Per unit it costs the same as one ladder (~–2/day mean mark-to-market) — you're just running three. It never has a naked short.

The short-first parallel version (no anchor) plants slightly more (3.22/day, 71% ≥3) and has a positive median day (+3.25) — and a –167 day (2024-12-18, hawkish FOMC, SPX –142, three unpaired short puts). That is Pandar's tail-selling book: "size so you're fine being assigned". One anchor covers one short; three parallel shorts means two are naked unless you buy three anchors — at which point you're paying three ATM puts of theta.

So, the sequence for 2–3 bombs on a normal day:
  1. Decide whether you're allowed to be naked short. If not → long-first ladders: buy 2–3 longs at adjacent 5-pt pairs around ~20Δ (or one at the 50) at the open or on a bounce, rest each sell $0.50–1.00 above its cost. One ~10-pt dip plants them all (67% of days for three). Leftover longs are your hedge; keep the resting sells working into the next day (nybhh's 90-DTE version is exactly this, slower).
  2. If you can carry short puts (PM, sized for assignment) → short-first ladders on a dip, rest each buy $0.50–1.00 below its short's price; positive-median days, but you own the –167 tail. Use one 50/20 anchor per short you want covered — the anchor is what makes short-first survivable, and it costs ~1.35/day of theta and drift.
  3. Either way, bomb the 50 last: once nothing is unpaired, rest STO K50–5 at anchor cost + 0.50; if a late dip fills it, your ATM put is a free ATM spread instead of a −3 to −8 line item. Or run the ATM strike as its own ladder — it plants on 6-pt wiggles.
  4. Don't expect the serial 50/20 to give three on a non-event day; its realistic yield is one, and it exists to keep you covered while short-first, not to maximize count.

5 · Pandar's version — the tail-vol cycle

PandarBear ran this on NVDA from ~Sept 2024 (a ~$1k Robinhood "meme account" plus buying-power top-ups → $530k by Dec 18 2024, +403% YTD screenshot; ~19k NVDA contracts at one point), then SPY/SPX into the Feb–Apr 2025 drawdown. His account of why it worked and the rules he actually stated:

  • The engine is single-name wing IV oscillating: "NVDA options expand and then crush… cycle between 'it's so over' and 'we're so back' relatively frequently", high volume, tight bid/ask. Deep-OTM $5-wide spreads that cost $0.05–0.10 in calm and $0.30+ in a scare.
  • He buys the long first (long puts on calm days, spread later), but tells everyone else to sell the tail first during expansion, "at a price/qty you'd be okay being assigned", then "aggressively take profit by buying the higher strike" when the tail crushes — "the most certain part of the trade is selling when tails are elevated". Result: a free (or credit) spread instead of a closed short.
  • Recognising an "expanded" tail is the core skill: underlying up, IV down, and the put still pricier than 2 days ago; 1-DTE far richer than further out; a specific expiry (e.g. Feb 7 2025 NVDA 190C) rich vs neighbours. He keeps the chain up all day and tracks "what spread width you can normally buy for $X"; bravo built a tracker of the strike where a $5-wide costs $0.10.
  • Sell weeklies up to the next monthly (they crush fastest); don't sell far-dated unless you want assignment; don't sell into earnings (a week before if at all).
  • Buy across strikes and expiries, no edge assumed on price; long legs at strikes a real black swan reaches (NVDA 90+ after 8/5/24; later 80–115P over 4/17 mopex).
  • Sizing/allocation: goal is index-beating returns with lower vol; "spend ~50% of gains above SPX YTD on hedges" so even if all expire worthless you still beat the index; add more the more you outperform, add less/stop when puts get expensive after a drop; keep PNR/EPR cushion.
  • Monetise: take profit on ITM/near-ITM spreads during a drop, redeploy to lower strikes ("nuke/Taiwan"), buy a few calls on the way down.
  • When it stops working — his own words: it "wouldn't have worked in 2023 because NVDA was on a roll" (puts too expensive, decayed fast); after the April 2025 V "market vol has been crushing too fast to outweigh the normal expansions NVDA experiences" (May 2025) — he stopped accumulating; by Aug 2025 he'd wait for "an extended period of low vol" then "Kelly in" because "something always eventually happens".

5 · Conditions for NVDA (or any single name), stated plainly

What must be true

  • Liquid, tight chain — NVDA's 5–10¢ markets at 5Δ are what let a 10¢-credit spread exist at all. Fees have to be ~zero (Pandar: free brokerage; 65¢/contract on a 10¢ spread is 6.5% each way).
  • Wing IV that expands and compresses often — a name that "cycles between 'so over' and 'we're so back'". Range-bound with scares is ideal; a one-way trend gives you only one leg (this sample: sell-first puts and buy-first calls worked; the mirrors never filled).
  • Deep OTM strikes that a real shock reaches (Pandar: NVDA 90 in Aug 2024 → buy 80–115P), $5-wide, current + next monthly for the longs, weeklies up to the next monthly for the shorts (fastest crush).
  • Not into earnings — Sep-18 here carries the Aug-26 event; Pandar doesn't sell tails into it and would sell the weekly before it, if anything.

What you're watching for

  • "Expanded" tail: stock up, IV down, and the far put still pricier than two days ago; one expiry rich vs its neighbours; 1-DTE rich vs further out. Sell it, size for assignment (≤10% NLV loss if the name goes to zero).
  • "Cheap" tail: the $5-wide back under 0.10 — buy the strike above your shorts (rest it at sale – 0.10 and let the wave fill it), or buy outright only if you already have inventory of paid-out spreads.
  • Call side only when the call wing is bid above ATM by several vols; otherwise buy far-OTM call spreads on puke days as the right-tail hedge.
  • Stop condition (Pandar, May 2025): when overall vol crushes faster than the name's wing expansions can recoup, stop accumulating and wait for an extended low-vol stretch to build again.
One-line version: on SPX the bomb is legged in minutes around 20Δ because the 5-wide is thin relative to the intraday wiggle; on NVDA the same structure is legged over hours-to-days at 5–15Δ because the 5-wide is fat and it's the wing IV, not the intraday delta, that swings the legs. Both are "sell the leg when it's rich, buy it when it's cheap, keep the spread you're left holding". The anchor/50-20 machinery is an SPX intraday device; on NVDA the backstop is sizing.

6 · Upside calls call side

The mirror structure

Call bomb = long Klo call / short Khi call, planted for a credit. Bullish tape: buy the call, then sell the further-OTM call into the rip. Bearish/neutral: sell the OTM call into a rip, then buy the nearer call on the pullback. Payoff: width × 100 if SPX settles above Khi.

Why it plants less often

  • Vanna is against you on both legs. The rip that should make Khi rich also drops IV; the pullback that should make Klo cheap lifts IV. Delta and vega fight each other, whereas on puts they add.
  • Flat/inverted call skew — the far call isn't overpriced the way the far put is, so there's less premium to sell.
  • Index up-moves are grinds, not gaps — the paper: call-side delta "increases more gradually", OTM call strikes get touched less, so both the intraday planting edge and the PoT-driven exit edge are smaller.

When calls do work (from the thread)

  • Call-skew events on single names: Jan 31 2025 NVDA — "call tails expanding a lot", specifically Feb 7 190C+ "most overpriced vs other expirations, likely to crush after the weekend"; Pandar sold naked, Cris "spammed" call sales, Kreisleriana turned NVDA short calls into spreads. Sell the far call into the grab, buy the nearer call once it collapses. Seb: MSTR call side. Rule stays: only when calls are overpriced; Pandar won't pay for long call spreads unless he's bullish.
  • Right-tail hedge, bought in panic (this is not legging — it's cheap convexity): Mar 20 2025 "buying call spreads on big down days for NVDA and SPX at the 6k–6.2k level, in case we moon and 'not long enough'"; Mar 21 "call spreads on various tickers further out to hedge against a 15–20%+ year"; Apr 2025 6500 SPX call spreads; then Aug 2025 "selling off the SPX 6500 spreads as we grind up, reinvesting in 7000+ call spreads and a small amount of 5800 put spreads 1 year out". Rationale (FiveDimeClive): if the risk-reversal premium holds, calls are structurally underpriced.
Practical read: for SPX, treat call bombs as opportunistic — plant them when the upside is what's bidding vol (squeeze days, "spot-up/vol-up" tape, mega-cap event skew), and otherwise use Pandar's approach: buy far-OTM call spreads with a slice of put-bomb profits on capitulation days, sell them into the V.

8 · Charlie McElligott's read

Cross-Asset Desk Note · Nomura · McElligottre: "Delta Bombs" — SPX put spreads legged for credit

Bottom line: this is a long-gamma-regime harvesting strategy that pays out in the short-gamma regime — the same dealer plumbing that lets you leg a put spread for a CREDIT in seven minutes is the plumbing that makes the eventual break violent enough to put your short strikes deep ITM. Which is elegant — and it's also exactly where the risk hides.

Mechanically what's happening: in a "long Gamma" tape (dealers long, spot pinned between the walls, sell-rips / buy-dips), SPX oscillates ±10/15 handles intraday with almost no drift, and vol wobbles with it — dip = IV bid, bounce = IV offered — so on the PUT side vanna and delta stack in your favor on BOTH legs, and a 5-wide at ~1σ that's "worth" ~75c can be crossed for a credit inside the noise. That's the edge — not the dime you keep — the edge is you're building INVENTORY of downside convexity while the regime is dampened, at zero carry, and the payoff is contingent on the regime flip: gamma flip breached → dealers short gamma → CTAs hit triggers → vol-control de-grosses over the next 3–5 sessions → LevETF rebal sells more the lower it goes → your 20Δ shorts get "touched" (PoT ~2× delta is just the polite way of saying the wings get visited when the tape goes one-way) → the ladder is worth 3–5x its width in mark before expiry.

The tell on the risk is IN the paper's own numbers — 7 minutes median to plant, 60 minutes median hold on the ones that failed. Translation: the failed plants are the days the tape STOPPED being two-way — you sold the Klo put on the "dip" that turned out to be the first leg of a real de-gross, or you bought the Khi put on the "bounce" that was a squeeze. So the leg risk isn't random noise — it's regime-shift risk concentrated into the exact minutes you're naked one leg. Manage it accordingly:

  • Plant inside the walls. Short-first only while spot is ABOVE the gamma flip / vol trigger and inside the put wall–call wall range; below the flip you're selling puts into "the dark side" and expecting a bounce that the plumbing is now working AGAINST. Long-first below the flip is fine — that's the leg you WANT to be stuck with.
  • Respect the clock. Don't initiate the short leg into the 3:30–4:00pm window on a down day (LevETF rebal is pro-cyclical — it sells more the lower it goes) or into an OpEx afternoon where charm/vanna are unwinding the pin. And DON'T sit in a failed plant for an hour hoping — that hour is where the "zero downside" claim goes to die.
  • The 50/20 anchor is a VaR-down. Correct instinct — you carry the ATM put so the interim short is always a wide debit spread, never naked, and the anchor's day P&L is your honest cost-of-planting. Only ever one unpaired short per anchor. And the replay in §4 says the quiet part: over 102 real days the anchor averaged –1.35/day against +0.53 of credits, and it swung +107 on the Dec-18-2024 hawkish-FOMC puke — so the anchor IS the hedge and the bombs are what you harvest around it, not the other way round.
  • Serial vs parallel (§4b). One anchor, one strike, one pair at a time is a THREE-oscillation bet — that's an FOMC afternoon, not a Tuesday. Pandar's version was always the parallel one: first legs resting on a LADDER of strikes so the single 10-handle wobble plants the lot — and note the 50Δ is the easiest strike on the board to bomb because delta-per-point is highest at the money; bomb it LAST, because the moment it's a 5-wide it stops being your VaR-down.
  • NVDA (§5b) is the tell on WHY single-name is different. The 5Δ call IV runs 3–6 vols OVER ATM all ten sessions — that's the right-tail grab, "Spot Up, Vol Up" in miniature — and the deep-OTM put spread goes 0.94 → 0.10 in eleven sessions of grind. On a single name the leg-in is a WING-VOL trade over days, not a gamma-scalp over minutes; the intraday 20Δ machinery is an SPX device, full stop.
  • Sizing = "spend the alpha." Pandar's rule (a slice of outperformance vs Spooz funds the hedge book) is the right frame — it makes the convexity book self-funding out of the same regime that's making you money, so you're accumulating hedges INTO the euphoria instead of chasing them after the puke, when skew's 100%ile and everyone's forced to grab.

On calls: the paper's right that the mirror image is weaker but it under-explains WHY — negative spot/vol correlation means the rip that should richen your short call is also OFFERING vol, and the pullback that should cheapen your long call is BIDDING it — vanna against you both ways, plus flat call skew, plus grind-not-gap price action. The exception is the regime I keep flagging: "Spot Up, Vol Up" — Call Skew at 100%ile, put skew dead, the ONLY vol bid is upside-grab in single-name mega-cap Tech (the Jan-31-2025 NVDA 190C episode in the thread is textbook) — THAT's when you sell the far call into the grab and buy the nearer strike when it "collapses under the weight of its own $Delta." And Pandar's OTHER call use is different and also right: buying 6000/6200/6500 SPX call spreads on puke days in Mar–Apr 2025 when nobody wanted upside is "the only tail we fear is the right-tail crash-UP" priced at 25%-OTM lottery levels — feed the ducks into the V, roll to 7000+.

Where the NVDA machine broke — May 2025 — is also the most instructive part of the thread: post-V, realized vol collapsed, vol-control re-levered ("slingshot"), wing IV stopped expanding enough on the scares to beat the decay, and the tail-cycle edge went away. 8d · Why it works — the option mechanics, twice

Cross-Asset Desk Note · McElligottre: what's actually moving when you leg a call spread for a credit (May 4–6 2026, NVDA 235/240)

Bottom line: you are exploiting the fact that two adjacent OTM strikes are almost the SAME option — near-identical delta, gamma, vega — so on a move their PRICES swing together by dollars while their DIFFERENCE (the spread you end up owning) barely moves. Separate the two fills in time and the level swing of one leg pays for the whole spread. Then the vol surface — vanna in a "Spot Up, Vol Up" tape — turbocharges the leg you're short into your limit.

  • Delta / gamma — the level swing. May 4 15:55: 235C 0.135Δ @ 1.81, 240C 0.107Δ @ 1.37, NVDA 198.4. Both legs are ~10–14 delta — a $1 move is 10–14¢ on EACH; the spread's delta is only the DIFFERENCE, 0.028Δ = 3¢ per dollar. And they're long gamma: every dollar up ADDS delta (by May 6 09:45 at 201.3 the 240C is ~0.15Δ). So the +$2.9 gap did +0.61 to the 240C (1.37 → 1.98) — twice what static delta says — while the spread only went 0.42 → 0.55. Your resting sell at 1.91 needed the 240C to gain 0.54; the spread you were left holding needed to gain nothing. That asymmetry IS the trade: the credit comes out of the leg's level move, the spread's own value is a rounding error against it.
  • Vega / vanna — the surface did half the work. The 240C carried ~9¢ of vega per vol point; the May 6 rip in a mega-cap AI name is the textbook GRAB — call skew went 78th → 98th percentile that week, the 15Δ call wing picked up ~2–3 vols. That's another +0.20–0.25 on the 240C for free: negative spot/vol correlation would have fought you (rip → vol offered), but "Spot Up, Vol Up" pays you on BOTH greeks. It's why the fill came at 09:45 on a $2.90 move instead of needing $5.
  • Why sell first is HARDER on the call side and buy first is easier — same mechanics in reverse: sell-first needs the K call to CHEAPEN below your K+5 sale, i.e. spot down and/or call vol down after a grab; buy-first needs K+5 to RICHEN above your K cost — delta, gamma AND vanna all point that way in a rip. On NVDA the ripping mornings are gaps (May 6 09:33/09:45), so the fill is instantaneous — you're never carrying the long into a chop.
  • What you're holding afterwards and why it inflates without ever going ITM. A 5-wide bull call spread is, to first order, 5 × the risk-neutral probability of finishing above ~237.5 plus a smile term. It has positive delta (~0.03 at entry, rising to ~0.15+ as spot approaches the strikes), positive gamma below the midpoint, LONG vega while OTM. So as NVDA walked 198 → 226 → 236, the market's probability of a >240 finish went from ~8% to ~45%: the spread went 0.42 → 1.55 → 2.25 — with the strikes still untouched. Its cap is 5.00 only at expiry AND deep ITM; at 35 DTE with spot AT 235 the fair value is ~2.2–2.5 because there's still a coin-flip of finishing below. That's the number you sell.
  • Why you sell it at the top and don't hold: theta and the mirror. OTM spread = short theta; the moment spot stops going up, delta works against you at the same 0.15/pt rate that made you money, vol comes off the wing (vanna reverses), and time drains it — 2.25 (May 14) → 0.68 (May 22) → 0.00 (Jun 18). Path dependence: this instrument's mark PEAKS when the probability peaks, not when the stock does; the crowd that bid the wing on May 14 (call skew 100%ile, RR 0%ile) is the crowd that pukes the wing on May 15–22.
  • The plumbing. Dealers are SHORT the upside gamma the grab creates in mega-cap Tech; a gap up forces them to buy delta, which lifts spot, which lifts the wing IV — the self-referential loop that makes "next ripping morning" fill your 240C limit in twelve minutes. The same loop unwinds in reverse — which is why every one of these bombs from May–July went to zero at expiry and only the one bought when calls were CHEAP (Jul 29, RR 75%ile) went near max.

So the mechanism is: two near-identical strikes → the LEG moves in dollars, the SPREAD moves in cents → buy the leg when both are cheap, sell the neighbour when both are rich (delta + gamma + vanna stacked in a spot-up/vol-up rip) → you're left owning "5 × probability of a >240 finish" for a credit → sell that probability when the tape has priced it highest, because theta and the mirror move take it back.

The same thing, from first principles

Look at the two things you traded. A "235 call" is a ticket that pays you a dollar for every dollar NVDA finishes above 235 on June 18; a "240 call" is the same ticket with the line drawn five dollars higher. On May 4 the stock was at 198, so both tickets were long shots — worth 1.81 and 1.37 — and here is the whole trick in one sentence: they are almost the same bet. Anything that makes NVDA more likely to reach 240 makes it almost exactly as much more likely to reach 235. So when NVDA jumped three dollars on May 6, both tickets got more valuable together — the 240 ticket went from 1.37 to 1.98, and the 235 ticket went from 1.81 to 2.52 — while the difference between the two tickets crept from 0.42 to only 0.55. You bought the 235 ticket on Monday for 1.81. On Wednesday morning you sold the 240 ticket for 1.91. Two different tickets, but you sold the cheaper one for more than you paid for the dearer one, because you sold it two days later when the whole neighbourhood had repriced. What you have left is "long 235, short 240" — a bet that pays at most five dollars — and you were paid ten cents to hold it. That's the "delta bomb": you never had to guess where NVDA would end up; you only needed the two tickets to get more expensive together, once, in the right order.

Now open up why the tickets moved as much as they did on a three-dollar move. A ticket that is far out of the money has a small "delta" — the fraction of the stock's move it inherits — about 0.13 here, so three dollars should have added forty cents. It added sixty. The extra came from two things you should be able to see without formulas. First, the delta itself grows as the stock climbs toward the line: after the jump the 240 ticket was catching 15¢ per dollar instead of 11¢ — the ticket accelerates (people call that gamma). Second, the price of a long-shot ticket depends on how wild people think the stock is, and on a morning when a big AI name gaps up, traders pay more for the far-away tickets — the imagined wildness went up together with the price (that's the "vol up" part; the size of that sensitivity is what's called vega). Both effects pushed the ticket you were selling toward your asking price faster than the stock alone would have. Test it: on a slow drift up with the wildness fading, the same three dollars would have added maybe thirty cents and your 1.91 would not have filled that morning. The mechanism needs a jump, not a drift — and on NVDA the mornings after a puke are jumps.

What are you holding afterwards, and why did it become worth two dollars when NVDA never got above 240? Think of the spread as an insurance policy that pays $5 if the stock finishes above 240, nothing if it finishes below 235, and something in between in between. Its price is, roughly, five dollars times how likely the market thinks the payoff is, with a little extra for uncertainty. On May 4 that likelihood was maybe eight percent — forty-some cents. By May 14, with the stock at 236 and five weeks to go, it was closer to a coin flip: about $2.25. Nobody had to be paid the five dollars; the probability was what you were holding, and it had gone from "unlikely" to "even money." You can sell a probability. And notice it stopped at $2.25, not $5, with the stock sitting right on the 235 line: half the futures still end below the line, so the policy can't be worth more than about half its maximum until time runs out. That is why the sensible thing was to sell it there.

The last piece is why it goes back to nothing if you hang on. Every day the stock doesn't move, a bit of the "maybe" leaks out — there are fewer days left for the jump to happen (theta). And the same machinery that inflated the policy on the way up deflates it on the way down: the delta that was adding 15¢ a dollar now subtracts 15¢ a dollar, and the wildness that traders paid up for on May 14 was the first thing they stopped paying for on May 15. That's what the record shows — 2.25 on May 14, 0.68 a week later, zero on June 18. So the honest summary you could say back to me: I bought one long-shot ticket, waited for the neighbourhood to reprice on a jump, sold the next ticket over for more than I paid, and was left holding a probability for free — and a probability is something you sell when it's high, not something you wait on. The simplification I've made is treating the two tickets as identical; they aren't quite (that 0.42-to-0.55 creep is the difference), and on a slow, calm stock the creep can eat the whole edge — which is exactly why this is done on things that jump.

10 · CIO memo — what the two "Pandar" programs would actually do

What we would be running

Program 1 — the tail-sale book with buybacks ("sell the scare, keep the free spread"). Every day the screen looks at one name (NVDA first) and asks: is the far wing of the front expiry unusually rich right now — the 5Δ option's implied vol sitting well above the at-the-money vol, high versus its own last year, and higher than the same wing one month out? When yes, we sell that far option (a put on a scare, a call on a squeeze) in the front weekly, in a quantity chosen so that if the stock actually reaches the strike we are still inside our margin cushion. The moment it's sold we rest a buy order on the strike one notch nearer, priced 5–10¢ under what we sold for. Two things can then happen: the scare fades (usual), the wing crushes, our buy fills — we've taken most of the premium and are left holding a $5-wide spread we were paid to own; or the scare extends, the buy doesn't fill, and we sit on a short far-OTM option sized to be held to expiry. Income comes from the crush; the leftover spreads are free lottery tickets that pay $500 each if the stock ever goes through them.

Program 2 — the penny-spread inventory ("buy the tail when nobody wants it"). On calm, complacent days (vol rank low, the wing flat), buy $5-wide spreads 25%+ out of the money in the current and next monthly, only when they cost 10¢ or less; hold them as inventory; rest sell orders at 3–5× cost. Most expire worthless (that's the cost of the hedge, sized as a slice of profits); some inflate 3–5× on a scare and get sold; once in a while the stock goes through them and the whole inventory pays 50×. This is the hedge book. It funds nothing; Program 1 funds it.

  • Program 1 looks like an insurance seller with a discipline for taking profit early.
