## CIO memo — what the first seven out-of-sample days tell us (plain terms)

**What we're doing, in one breath.** We buy a put spread on the S&P 500 that pays off if the market drops in the next few weeks — and we try to get *paid* $10 to own it instead of paying for it. We do that by buying one leg first and selling the other leg a dime richer, then waiting for the market to come to our price. When the second leg fills, we've "planted a bomb": a free ticket that's worth up to $500 if the market falls through our strikes. When it doesn't fill within an hour, we're stuck holding one naked leg and have to close it, usually at a loss. Seven days of fresh data, never seen by the system before: six bombs planted, three failed attempts, cash P&L −$360, and the six planted bombs are currently worth about $585 on paper. The paper number is nice but doesn't count — those bombs are all bets on the same thing (a drop in September) and they can all go to zero together.

**The main thing we learned.** The system's "buy first" entry works when there is real, heavy put-buying underway — the kind that keeps pushing put prices up, so our sell order gets hit. On the two days that had that kind of selling pressure, it planted five bombs in a row without a miss. On three other days it fired on *faint* selling pressure that turned out to be a morning dip in a rally, bought the low, and ate three losses — one of them $300, which is double the loss ceiling we wrote down before the test started. So the picture is: the idea is right, the trigger is too eager. It cannot yet tell "the sellers are in control" from "a dip that's about to bounce."

**What Charlie and the outside reviewer warned us about.** Seven days is not proof. The five good fills came from just two days; the three losses came from three days. That's really "two good days vs. three bad days," not eight independent trades. The tempting fix — require heavier selling pressure before entering — was spotted *after* looking at the results, which is exactly how you fool yourself. The right move is to write that fix down now as a rule we will *watch* but not *use*, keep running the frozen system unchanged, and only promote the fix if it proves itself on days we haven't seen yet. They also said the $300 loss is not a footnote: whatever we decide, the next version of the test has to treat a loss above $150 as a formal failure, not an "interesting observation."

**Options and recommendation.** (1) Change the trigger now — rejected: it would contaminate the test we designed and we'd never know if the change or the luck did the work. (2) Stop the test and redesign — premature; the losses are within the tail we already flagged, and the wins are exactly where the thesis said they'd be. (3) *Recommended:* keep the frozen system running as-is through the live test, register three "shadow" rules today — the heavier-flow trigger, the wider credit ladder ($0.10/$0.20/$0.30 tracked side by side), and a scorecard of the sell-first trades the below-Vol-Trigger rule blocked — and let each one earn or lose its place on fresh data with the evidence bars already written. Expected timeline: 2–4 weeks of daily captures. Nothing touches real money until then.

## A Feynman explanation — what is actually going on here

Let's start with what the bet is, because everything else is bookkeeping. Options on the S&P 500 are prices people pay for insurance against a fall. We want to own a small piece of that insurance — a spread that pays if the index drops about 4% in a few weeks — but we'd like someone else to pay us for the privilege of holding it. That sounds impossible, and most of the time it is. The trick is timing: option prices wiggle minute to minute, and the two legs of our spread don't wiggle in perfect lockstep. If we buy one leg and then, a few minutes later, the other leg gets bid up more than the first one did, we can sell it for a dime more than we paid. Pocket $10; keep the insurance for free. That's the whole game. The question is only: *when* does the second leg get bid up?

Here is the physics of it. The put we sell gets bid up when lots of people are buying puts at once — and, importantly, when the *dealers* who sold them those puts have to hedge by selling the index, which pushes prices down, which makes more people buy puts. That's a feedback loop, and while it runs, put prices march upward and our sell order gets hit. HIRO is just a way of watching that loop in real time: it estimates how hard the dealers are being pushed to sell. On two of our seven days the loop was clearly running — a strong, sustained push — and the system planted five bombs cleanly. On three days the push was faint, the kind of reading you'd get from noise, and the market was actually about to rally. The system bought the bottom of a dip, the puts got cheaper, and an hour later it had to sell one leg at a loss. Notice the wins and losses weren't random: they line up with whether the feedback loop was really running.

Now here's where you have to be honest with yourself, because this is where people go wrong. It is very tempting to say "aha — just require a strong push before entering" and declare the problem solved. But I *found* that rule by looking at the answers. If you flip a coin eight times and then invent a rule that explains the pattern you saw, your rule will always look brilliant on those eight flips. Worse, our five wins came from only two days — so the "rule" might really be saying "it worked on days the market was falling," which is not much of a discovery. One of the five winners actually had a *faint* reading and won anyway. The only way to know whether the rule is real is to write it down now, precisely, then watch what it would have done on days that haven't happened yet — without letting it touch the live system. If it's real, it'll show up in the fresh data. If it's a coincidence, the fresh data will quietly bury it. Either outcome is useful; only the honest procedure gets you there.

One last thing about the $585 "paper profit." The six bombs we're holding are all bets on the same event — a September decline. If that decline comes, they pay together; if it doesn't, they expire together, worth nothing. So $585 is not six independent gains, it's one gain counted six times, and it isn't real until the calendar runs out. The number I'd actually watch is dull: cash in, cash out, and whether the failed attempts stay small. Right now the failures cost more than the successes earn — one $300 miss ate thirty successful plants. The thesis says that's fixable by being pickier about when the loop is really running. The data says: maybe. Ask me again in twenty signals.
