## Brent Kochuba

In positive gamma, dealers tend to buy weakness and sell strength. An aligned HIRO turn upward after a 3-point pullback should modestly raise the odds of a 3–7-point rebound, limit further pullback depth, and reduce the carried short put’s ordinary adverse path—but the edge should be strongest at the turn, before the rebound exhausts dealer demand. Once HIRO is already steep and loud, positive-gamma supply can absorb the buying: price has moved, dealers sell into it, and the short put may retain reversal risk without much remaining upside.

In negative gamma, dealers hedge with the move: buying as spot rises and selling as it falls. Early aligned upside can therefore produce faster, larger continuation, but failure is more dangerous—a renewed decline forces additional selling, deepens the pullback, and fattens the short put’s tail. Spot-up/vol-up would be especially unstable: the rally may be mechanically powerful, yet the regime remains stressed.

For a sell-first bomb, HIRO is not a standalone predictor. Use the first synchronized turn from a trough, combined with an actual price pullback, as a state and leg-order filter; use a mature steep/high-volume run as a veto against entering late. The corrected results say “early alignment plus geometry,” not “more HIRO slope is always better.”

Ranked explanations:

1. Phase/geometry error: the steep state usually occurred near the end of the move, whereas the edge lived at the trough turn after a real pullback.
2. Positive-gamma absorption: dealer selling into strength truncates continuation once the rebound is mature.
3. HIRO’s coincident, partly price-following nature: it confirms active hedging pressure but does not independently forecast the next 15 minutes.
4. Five-day, down-drift sample: important uncertainty, but it cannot explain the within-sample contrast between early and late entry.
5. Execution and episode dependence: minute-level counts can exaggerate confidence unless clustered by independent run.

The most informative next check is a prospective, episode-level test that freezes one executable entry rule: first aligned moderate turn from a trough after a ≥3-point pullback, versus entry 5–10 minutes later in the same run, stratified by positive and negative gamma. Compare +5/30, +5/60, maximum adverse excursion, and actual spread-credit feasibility.

Really? No—I am not saying no high-probability bomb exists; I am saying the bomb is planted at the early synchronized turn after the pullback, not after HIRO is already steep and obvious. In positive gamma the late loud state is often the exit signal or entry veto, while in negative gamma the opportunity may be stronger but the failed-trade tail is materially worse.

## Charlie McElligott

“Price is news” is the key distinction. HIRO measures an estimated basket hedging flow, and your correlations show that the loud one- to fifteen-minute print largely travels with spot; by the time it looks spectacular, the mechanical impulse may already be in the price.

In positive gamma, dealer rebalancing is countercyclical: weakness elicits buying and strength elicits selling. The first coordinated HIRO turn after a pullback can identify the point where hedging flow, discretionary dip-buying, and perhaps short-horizon systematic demand synchronize, increasing rebound probability and cushioning the short put. But a steep, high-volume mature run invites absorption and profit-taking; continuation falls while reversal exposure remains.

In negative gamma, dealer flow becomes procyclical. Upside alignment can cascade through dealer hedging, short covering, and—over longer horizons—CTA or vol-control demand, producing shallower pullbacks and stronger continuation. Yet if the signal fails, the feedback loop reverses: deeper downside, higher volatility, and a much uglier carried-short-put tail. Spot-up/vol-up is not clean reassurance; it can signal stressed convexity and unstable upside chasing.

Ranked explanations for the results:

1. Signal timing: “all lines just turned together from a trough” captures flow acceleration; “steep and loud” captures a crowded, mature price move.
2. Pullback geometry: selling optionality after a 5–8-point reset offers room for reflexive recovery; entering later sacrifices that cushion.
3. Positive-gamma absorption: dealers mechanically monetize the rally, suppressing follow-through.
4. Price-following flow: HIRO confirmation is contemporaneous information, so extrapolating slope confuses confirmation with forecast.
5. Sample/regime: five down-drift, positive-gamma sessions limit generalization, particularly to negative-gamma or vol-shock environments.
6. Other systematic flows: CTA and vol-control may matter, but their slower signals should not be inferred from HIRO alone without direct measures.

The single best next check is an out-of-sample, phase-conditioned event study: freeze the early-turn/pullback trigger, compare it with delayed entries inside the identical run, and interact both with dealer-gamma sign and spot/vol direction. Use independent episodes—not minutes—and make maximum adverse excursion co-primary with fill probability.

Really? There appears to be a tradable short-horizon setup, but it is the early “flow inflection plus pullback” setup—not the visually irresistible steep HIRO trend. Chase the loud state and you are often buying yesterday’s news with a short-convexity liability; enter at the synchronized turn and the current evidence says the odds improve, although five sessions do not yet justify “high probability.”
