### 1.0 Findings and implications (verbatim)

What the 1-min trend-catching work found (SPX, 485 gate days + 107 below-VT days)

1. Trend is not detectable early enough to act on. 09:35–09:45 classifiers (OR breakout, VWAP persistence, EMA stack, drift-vs-VT) have OOF AUC 0.52–0.61; only by noon 0.78–0.86 — descriptive, not tradeable. The one 09:35-detectable thing (the "popper", move935 ≥ θ) is already priced the moment you see it.
2. Every ride/trailing-stop rule captured ≈ 0. On S1 grind days the true peak was +12 pts, but the grind's own ~10–12 pt (≈2.5 OR-spread) pullbacks whipsawed every trailing exit; >50% of exits sold below the 09:45 level. Below-VT: 61/85 sub-par days had a genuine ≥70 bp up-leg (median 92 bp) and three independent approaches — churn, hold, wait-for-clean-alligator — all captured −2% of it; the clean entries landed 60% up the day's range with 33 bp left. Verdict quote: "a wide/steep/clean alligator here is an exhaustion signal, not a continuation one → if anything the edge is in FADING it."
3. The one live remnant was a 09:45 veto (VT held on a closing basis, Call Wall respected — WHETHER to trade, not WHEN), and sizing on the popper (1.5× on detected up-context days), never timing.

What that means for the delta bomb — it's confirmation, and it hands you the placement rule

- The bomb is a fade structure (sell the put when the down-swing is spent, buy when the up-swing is spent). The trend work says the 1-min SPX tape is mean-reverting at exactly the amplitude you need (10–12 pt pullbacks against 3–7 pt legs), and that "clean trend" on 1-min is where moves die. So Rule 1 isn't a heuristic — it's the empirical result of a whole worktree that tried the opposite and lost.
- Use the roll-over triggers as leg triggers, mirrored: sell leg = down-move stalled: no new session low for 3–5 one-min bars + close back above the 5-bar typical-price mean/VWAP (or a failed probe of a level, e.g. VT/Put Wall) → sell the put at the bid now and rest the buy at sale − 0.10. Buy leg = the mirror (no new high for 3–5 bars + close back below TPM, or a failed Call-Wall probe). This replaces "park a number and hope" with "act at exhaustion", which is what the paper's 7-minute plants actually were.
- Use the veto for whether to leg at all: Brent's band position — pos = (O−VT)/(CW−VT) ≥ 0.7 or room_to_CW ≤ 0.5 → up-move capped → sell-first (which needs the bounce) is the worse order; long-first is fine. VT lost on a closing basis → don't open an unpaired short. Live efficiency = |close−open|/path over the last 60 bars > ~0.5 → one-way tape, hold off; < ~0.3 → chop, go.
- The 60-minute rule is cheap: from my touch table, 4 bp completes 80% within 60 min vs 86% within 120 — the second hour buys only +6 pts of completion while carrying the whole adverse tail. Paper's rule stands.
- Don't try to time direction with these tools — the worktree proved the ORACLE value exists (+$2k/yr) but is locked behind a sign nobody can read at decision time. Bombs don't need it; they need the retrace, which is the 85% event.

### 1.1 CIO memo — in simple terms (revised after Codex review)

**What we looked at.** Our own `spy_chaser` research spent a full cycle trying to catch intraday SPX trends on 1-minute bars (485 positive-gamma "gate" days plus 107 below-Vol-Trigger days). It failed in a specific and useful way: whether the day will trend cannot be read at the open (the classifiers barely separate trend from chop until noon), every trailing-stop or "ride the move" rule was shaken out by the market's own 10–12 point pullbacks, and on the below-Vol-Trigger days three different approaches — trade often, hold, or wait for a clean trend — each captured about nothing of the moves that were there, because by the time a trend looked clean most of it was spent. The only things that survived were a veto (do not trade when the Vol Trigger fails on a closing basis) and position sizing — never timing.

**Why that matters for the SPX delta bomb.** The bomb is the mirror-image trade. It does not need SPX to trend; it needs SPX to move about 3–7 points, because we sell the put on a dip and buy the strike above on the bounce (or the reverse). Measured on 845 sessions of 1-minute SPX data (16,055 attempts), a 3-point move in the needed direction arrives within 60 minutes 78–80% of the time and within two hours 84–86% of the time — the same up or down — so the process does not require being right on direction. Two honest caveats: those are moves in the index, not option fills, and the full cycle (one leg fills, then the next opposite leg's move also arrives) is 56% within an hour and 68% within two. What the trade does require is surviving the path: in about 17% of attempts SPX first runs 15+ points against the open leg (6–7% run 30+ points) before giving us the move we need, and that is where the losses in this strategy live.

**What changes in how we trade it.** Two rules are settled by data, two are hypotheses we will trade cautiously and test. Settled: (1) no ATM "anchor" put — in the 102-day replay it cost about $127 per bomb against ~$50 of credit and is the whole reason the first live bomb lost money; (2) one unpaired leg at a time, the paper's 60-minute clock, and a hard cap on any unpaired short (bought back or capped when it is 3.5 points against us, about 15 SPX points) — the second hour adds only ~6 points of probability that the needed move arrives while carrying the entire adverse tail, and a 30-point excursion costs roughly $630–800 per contract, which the small credits cannot pay for; only the spread's exit or hedge value can. Hypotheses: (3) place each leg at exhaustion, using the roll-over detectors the trend work already built with the sign flipped (down-move stalls → sell the put; up-move stalls → buy the higher strike); (4) use the gamma-band position to choose which leg goes first (pressed to the Call Wall → long-first) and a 1-minute close below the Vol Trigger as the veto on opening any unpaired short.

**Decision.** Hold as operating rules for discretionary 1–3 lot trading; do not promote to a systematic program. The anchor question is closed (drop it). The exhaustion-trigger claim is mechanistically supported but not yet measured for this trade — the one experiment worth funding is `scripts/spx_legin_ev.py` (about an hour on data already local): the same touch table conditioned on "entered at a stall trigger" versus "entered anytime", fit on 2022–2024 and held out on 2025–2026, reporting P(needed move within 60 min) and P(adverse > 20 bp). Adoption is pre-registered: the trigger stays only if it improves one of those two numbers by at least 5 percentage points out of sample; otherwise it is dropped and we keep the settled rules — a high-hit-rate process with a managed tail, not a directional call.

### 1.2 The mechanism in plain terms — a Feynman-style explanation

Let's not start with the names. Look at one thing: a one-minute chart of the S&P 500 on an ordinary day. It does not go anywhere in a straight line. It goes up ten points, gives back seven, goes up eight, gives back nine. If you slow it down and measure — and we did, 845 days of it — a three-point wiggle in whichever direction you happen to need shows up within an hour about four times out of five, and it does not care which way you were leaning. Now, the trend-catching project asked the opposite question of the same chart: can I ride the ten-point moves? Every rule it tried got shaken off by the seven-point give-backs, and by the time a move looked "clean" enough to trust it was mostly over. So the chart told us something in two different ways at once: at this scale it wiggles more than it walks.

Here is why that is exactly what the delta bomb needs. The bomb is two orders that only make sense as a pair: sell a put when the market has just dipped (puts are a little dearer then), and buy the put five points nearer to the money when the market has just bounced (puts are a little cheaper then). The two prices differ by about sixty cents at any instant, so to buy the dearer strike for no more than you sold the cheaper one, the whole put ladder has to sag by sixty-odd cents between your two orders — which is about a three-point rise in the index. That is all. You are not predicting where the S&P closes; you are waiting for one three-point wiggle, and the chart hands those out generously. Direction is not the trade. The wiggle is.

So where does the money get lost? Not on the pair — a finished pair is free by construction. It is lost in the gap between the two orders, when you are holding one leg alone. Four times in five the wiggle comes quickly and nothing happens. But about one time in six the market first walks fifteen points the wrong way, and one time in fourteen it walks thirty, before it comes back — and while it walks, your lone leg bleeds, and your resting second order sits further away than ever. That is the whole risk in one sentence: the trade fails not because you were wrong about direction, but because you had to wait, and waiting occasionally costs more than many finished pairs are worth. That is why the rules are about the wait, not the view: one lone leg at a time, a clock on it, a price at which you give up, and no expensive "anchor" put bought to feel safe during the wait — that anchor cost more than the pair it was guarding.

Here is how you could catch me if this picture were wrong. If the index at one-minute scale trended more than it wiggled, the touch table would show the second leg arriving rarely and the adverse excursions dominating — it shows the reverse. If placing legs "at exhaustion" (right after a small move stalls) really helps, then attempts entered at those moments should complete more often, or get run over less, than attempts entered at random minutes; we have not measured that yet, so it stays a hypothesis with a pre-registered test, and if the numbers do not move, we drop it. And here is what I have simplified: the sixty-cent gap is not fixed — it grows when volatility rises and shrinks when it falls, so "three points" is today's number, not a law. The sentence to say back is: I am not betting on where the market goes; I am selling one leg on a dip and buying the other on a bounce, the bounce almost always comes, and the entire job is to make sure the rare times it doesn't come quickly are small.

## 2. The signal stack for planting quickly — assembled from the 1-min evidence (verbatim, 2026-08-18)

Here's the stack, assembled only from what the 1-min work actually measured. Everything below is "confirmed turn", not "knife" — the one lesson that repeated in every worktree (trend timing, VT-reclaim, bottom-detection): **precise-extreme callers were more precise and made less; the confirmed reclaim paid.**

### The signal stack for planting quickly (SPX, 1-min)

**0. Units first (so thresholds don't drift with vol)**
- `u = max(0.10 × IM, 4 pts)` — the "ruler" from the trend toolkit; grind pullbacks measured ≈ 2.5 u (10–12 pts). Your leg needs ~0.75 u. Alternative unit: `ORspread` = 09:30–09:34 range.
- ATR(1-min) for bar-shape tests; IM from `im_estimator.py`; VT/CW/PW from `spotgamma_fixed`.

**1. Regime & veto (decides WHETHER and WHICH LEG FIRST — the only things the trend work found survivable)**
- Veto on any unpaired short: a 1-min **close below VT** (toolkit's live remnant). No SG levels → long-first only.
- Band position `pos=(O−VT)/(CW−VT)`, `room=(CW−O)/IM`: pos ≥ 0.7 or room ≤ 0.5 → up-move capped → **long-first**; 0.25 < pos < 0.7 & room ≥ 1 → either order; pos ≤ 0.25 (sitting on VT) → coin-flip tape, smallest size.
- Time window **10:00–14:00**: first touches before 2 pm whip (median 4 episodes, only 25% commit); the last ~1.5 h is where pokes become breaks (25–34% of first touches, single sustained move). Don't open an unpaired leg after ~14:30.
- Below-VT/sg<0 days: more oscillation ("the close round-trips") but fatter adverse tail (>40 bp 14% vs 10%) → same rules, half size.

**2. Setup — the swing must be *spent*, not starting (arm the leg)**
- Run from the last swing extreme ≥ **2 u** (≈ 8–12 pts) or ≥ 0.2 IM — that's the measured pullback amplitude; below that you're fading noise, above 3 u you're in a trend leg (efficiency test below).
- **Mature alligator in the direction you're about to fade** — the v3 gate run as an *exhaustion* detector (its own verdict): `e5<e9<e20`, `(e20−e5)/ATR ≥ 0.8`, 5-bar steepness ≥ 0.5, ≥ 90% of last 10 closes below e5, clean bodies (mean body/range ≥ 0.5). When that prints on a down-move, the sell-put setup is armed; mirror (`e5>e9>e20`) arms the buy-leg.
- Chop confirmation over the last 10–20 bars: Kaufman ER(10) < ~0.3 or ≥ 4 VWAP flips → oscillating tape (both legs will fill); ER > 0.5 over 60 bars → one-way tape, stand down.

**3. Trigger — the confirmed turn (fire the leg)** — need 2 of 3, all price-only, all from the toolkit/BVT scripts:
- **Stall:** no new session/swing low for **3–5 one-min bars** (Chart-Analyst roll-over rule, mirrored).
- **Reclaim:** 1-min close back above the 5-bar TPM ((H+L+C)/3 mean) or e5, and/or a lower-low that fails by < 0.15 ORspread (the "lower swing high" rule inverted).
- **Reversal bar / failed probe:** bullish version of the 5-min "brutal reversal" (close>open, range ≥ 1.2×ATR, body/range ≥ 0.55, close > prior-2-bar high) **or** a failed probe of a level — low within 0.1–0.25 IM of VT/Put Wall/round strike then close back above (dealer-buy-the-dip signature; pokes are median 2/day, ~7 pts deep, revert ≈ breach by depth so don't try to read depth).
- Optional vol tell (needs the 1-min IV legs, §35/§36): put-skew **steepening into the flush** → bounce more likely (ρ +0.51); ATM IV **still rising** → stays down (ρ −0.37). If ATM IV is still rising, don't fire.

**4. Execution (the paper's rules with the numbers attached)**
- On trigger: **sell the put at the bid immediately**, rest the buy 5 higher at sale − 0.10. Clock 60 min (one-way move arrives 80% in 60 vs 86% in 120 — the second hour isn't worth the tail). Cap: unpaired short ≥ sale + 3.5 (~15 pts) → cap with the next-lower strike or buy back. One unpaired leg. No anchor.
- **Buy leg = the exact mirror** at the top of the bounce: no new high 3–5 bars + close below TPM/e5, or bearish brutal-reversal bar, or failed CW probe (tag CW − 0.1·IM, no new high 3 bars).
- After a fill, re-anchor the next resting limit to the neighbour's current ask + c, not to the last number.

**5. Anti-signals (what the evidence says to ignore or fear)**
- Don't classify direction at 09:35–09:45 (AUC 0.52–0.61); don't use EMA crosses as a trend engine (demoted in the toolkit); don't wait for the "clean" alligator to *follow* it (captured −2%).
- Fast early mover (`emove ≥ 5u` by 09:50) = the day's pop is already priced; treat the first hour as veto/observation, not planting.
- A brutal-reversal bar *against* your open leg = exit/cap now, don't wait for the clock.

**Status:** every component is measured on its original question; the *combination as a bomb trigger* is not — that's `scripts/spx_legin_ev.py` (trigger = stall + reclaim; baseline = anytime; fit 2022–24, hold out 2025–26; adopt only if P(one-way move in 60 min) +5 pp or P(adverse > 20 bp) −5 pp). All inputs are local: SPX 1-min OHLC (853 d), SPY 1-min w/ volume for VWAP, SG levels, IM estimator, and the BVT/toolkit scripts named above.

### 5.5 CIO memo — HIRO for SPX delta-bomb leg timing (2026-08-19, simple terms)

**What we tested and found.** We asked whether SpotGamma's HIRO — its live estimate of options-driven dealer hedging pressure for the S&P 500 basket — can tell us when to put on each leg of an SPX delta bomb (a 5-wide put spread legged in for zero or credit; the second leg fills when SPX moves about 3–7 points the right way, and the risk is the lone leg while we wait). We had five sessions of HIRO (Aug 12–18), all positive-gamma, narrow-range days, joined to real SPX 1-minute prices, 1,630 minute-starts. Two questions were asked in sequence: does the *state* of flow (strong up, strong down, quiet; 5/15/30-minute windows; calls and puts agreeing; all-trades and next-expiry agreeing) change the odds of the needed move, and — the trader's real question — does an *established, smooth* HIRO trend let us fill directionally in the middle of it. Everything below is exploratory: five days, one gamma regime, overlapping minutes.

**What worked.** One thing, consistently: HIRO told us which leg was dangerous to hold alone. When all-trades and next-expiry flow were both strongly negative (puts being bought, calls sold), a carried short put was run 15+ points against us 18% of the time — 30% in the late-morning block — versus 5–7% otherwise; a carried long put had essentially no adverse run in positive flow. In the trend experiment the same asymmetry appeared: a smooth *negative* HIRO trend did carry price, and long-first on a bounce filled 60–70% of the time with zero adverse excursions (best cells 0.70 vs 0.27 baseline; every EMA span agreed), and the worst flow reversal of the week — Aug 17, 11:35, −2.8 billion in 15 minutes — was exactly the moment a second short put would have been carried into the hole. Quiet HIRO was the best state for both legs filling, which fits the wider finding that the tape's own oscillation, about 80% per hour, is the only edge in the fill itself.

**What didn't, and what kind of did.** Using HIRO *direction* to get the up-move we need failed, in both framings. Strong up-flow was followed by *fewer* 3-point up-moves than quiet flow, and a smooth positive HIRO trend (our pre-registered cell: 30-minute persistence, not turned over, calls and puts agreeing, price confirming) filled sell-first on pullbacks 6% of the time for a 5-point move in 30 minutes against 42% at random minutes; across 1,788 sweep cells the average effect was −14 points and the best was +6 — noise. A plain price-only trend state did better than the HIRO cell, which suggests that on positive-gamma days HIRO upside flow is absorbed by dealers and mostly relabels a price trend we could already see. HIRO as a turn trigger was worse than price. The "kind of worked" column is the down branch and the risk veto: real-looking, but three days and ~50 entries, confounded with two down days, and measured on a spot-move proxy rather than actual option fills. Both reviewers — Brent (mechanics) and Charlie (flows), here and via Codex — agreed on that split and on the reason: in positive gamma, upside flow gets faded and downside de-grossing persists.

**Decision and next steps.** No change to how we trade: the five-line playbook stands (gate, leg order, arm, stall as discipline, one leg / 60-minute clock / buy-back cap / no anchor). HIRO enters only as a provisional veto — no naked short put while all-trades and next-expiry 15-minute flow are both strongly negative — and as a preference for quiet flow when planting; it is not a direction or timing signal. The experiment is frozen as v2 (episode-level units, option-level fill endpoint on SPXW quotes, paired price-only control, shuffled-HIRO control, out-of-sample sessions only) with a pass bar per direction after at least 20 independent episodes and 15 sessions. Two operational requirements follow: the HIRO capture must run every day (the product retains only five sessions), and SPXW 1-minute quotes must be pulled per session so the endpoint is a real fill. Cost is small; the prize is a validated "which leg to carry" rule and possibly a down-trend long-first play, both of which would only pay for themselves on the day it matters.

### 5.6 Independent Feynman-style explanation (written from the evidence files only, not from 5.5)

Start with one minute. Say it is 11:14 on one of the five days and SPX has just closed that minute at some price. The experiment asks two plain questions: in the next 15, 30, or 60 minutes, did the index trade 3, 5, or 7 points higher (that is the "fill" for someone who sold a put first and needs a bounce to buy the other put), and before it got there, how far did it sink the wrong way (the "adverse" move against the lone leg)? The other side asks the mirror: did it trade that many points lower, and how far did it rise first? Each minute then gets a label from what the HIRO line did over the previous 15 to 60 minutes: climbing steadily with few wobbles (UP), falling steadily (DOWN), loud in either direction, or nothing special. The fill rate inside a label is compared with a baseline built from all 1,630 minutes at the same clock times across the five days.

Open it up. If rising HIRO meant dealers were being pushed to buy, UP minutes should fill the upside leg more often and bruise the short leg less. The numbers went the other way. In the pre-registered cell (33 minutes, three days), selling first during a smooth HIRO climb and a 3-point pullback got the 3-point bounce within 15 minutes 12% of the time against a 48% baseline, and 5 points within 30 minutes 6% against 42%; the chance of a 15-point bruise was unchanged (6% vs 7%). Across every up-trend definition tried, fills averaged 14 points below baseline; the best was 6 above. Loud flow rhymed: strong flow either way brought fewer up-touches and more down-touches over the next hour, and strong down-flow tripled the odds a short-first leg sat 20 bp underwater (18% vs 5.5%). Only the mirror looked friendly: falling HIRO slope lifted down-fills for a long-first leg to 43–61% against 38–42%, with almost no adverse. Downward pressure followed through; rising HIRO read like a bounce being faded.

Now test it; this could be the calendar, not the signal. Three of the five days drifted lower with HIRO ending negative, so any "down" label inherits a tailwind and any "up" label a headwind. The controls sharpen that worry. A price-only trend, same geometry but built from SPX closes alone, lifted fills both ways (40% vs 16% baseline up, 69% vs 46% down), so a plain price trend carried information that HIRO-on-top-of-price did not add. The clock-matched baseline matters too: the needed 3 to 4 point move arrives in most hours anyway (63 to 69% of minutes here, about 80% per hour over 815 days), so a signal must beat that, not zero. The sweep ran 3,456 cells; the best down cells are the same 47 minutes relabeled under flags that never bound, and the median down lift was one point. What would change my mind: a cell that survives new sessions, especially wide-range or negative-gamma days, and beats the price-only control. Rerun both scripts; read the per-day rows first.

Here is what was simplified. A "fill" is SPX spot touching a level, not an option trading at a price; real put fills depend on quotes and volatility and can be better or worse than spot says. Five days is five coin flips, all positive-gamma with 19 to 38 point ranges, and consecutive minutes overlap, so 33 UP minutes are a handful of episodes on three days, not 33 independent trials, and the DOWN cell had seven entries. HIRO is an S&P basket, not cash SPX, and with thousands of sweep cells the best one is partly luck. I did not look at option prices or at any day when dealers were short gamma, where the sign could flip. The handle: on these five quiet days, a strong or steadily rising HIRO line did not help the second leg fill; if anything it marked moments when price leaned down, so selling the first leg into rising HIRO was the one clear mistake, while falling HIRO helped the long-first side a little.

## 6. Rule set and dashboard — "early synchronized HIRO turn on a pullback" (2026-08-19)

**Rules (computable at the close of minute t; act at the next bar's open).** L = cumulative `all` total ($B) since 09:30, Lc/Lp call/put parts, N = cumulative `nextExp` total. Trough-anchored run: running low of L; run high = max L since that low; if L falls ≥ 0.6 $B from the run high the run is broken and both reset at t. run = L(t) − L(trough); dur = minutes since trough; rate = run/dur × 60. **Fire** when all hold: (1) dur ≥ 10; (2) rate ≥ 2 $B/hr (rate ≥ 4 with 30-min flow ≥ 1 $B = steep/late: no new entry); (3) ΔC > 0 and ΔP > 0 since the trough with min/max ≥ 0.25; (4) ΔN > 0 and ΔN/run ≥ 0.5; (5) drawdown of L from the run high < 0.6; (6) 30-bar rolling high of closes − close ≥ 3 pts; (7) 09:35–15:45 (15:00 for the 60-min horizon). Execution: sell the put at the next open, rest the buy 5 higher at sale − 0.10, 60-min clock, buy back at sale + 3.5, one unpaired leg, one entry per episode. Exit/veto: run broken (flow shut-off) while unpaired → cover; steep/late state without pullback → no entry.

### 6.2 Codifying the Charlie gates — formulas, and what survived testing (2026-08-21)

**Formulas (causal, at fire minute t; run/trough/peak from §6):** `rate_now = (L(t)−L(t−5))/5×60`; `rate_prev = (L(t−5)−L(t−10))/5×60`; `accel = rate_now − rate_prev`; `resp_full = (SPX(t)−SPX(trough))/run` (pts/$B); `resp_recent = (SPX(t)−SPX(t−5))/max(L(t)−L(t−5), 0.05)`; `responding = resp_recent ≥ 0.5×resp_full`; `giveback = (L_peak − L(t))/run`. Steep gate: fire allowed iff ¬steep ∨ (accel > 0 ∧ giveback < 0.15 ∧ responding). Quality score Q = [min(ΔC,ΔP) ≥ 0.15] + [dur ≥ 15 ∧ cons ≥ 0.6] + [accel ≥ 0] + [giveback < 0.15], trade Q ≥ 3. Early invalidation: cancel if L drops ≥ 0.3 $B below the fire level (or the run breaks) in minutes t+1…t+3 before the +3 touch. Context tags: RV percentile, time bucket, CW distance in IM.

**Result on the 32 episodes:** archetypes resolve perfectly (Aug-18 14:12 passes: accel +3.3, giveback 0.09, responding; Aug-12 10:29 blocked: steep, accel −3.3, giveback 0.30, not responding, invalidated). But as rules: `steep_ok` admitted **zero** steep fires (by the time "steep" is true at 1-min resolution, acceleration has already rolled over — the ignition phase *is* the pre-steep early turn; Aug-18's steep winners were re-fires of runs whose early fire had already won at 10:22/11:46); the **Q score inverts** (Q ≥ 3: 0.40 win5 on 2 days; Q ≤ 1: 0.62 on 5 days) because `q_persist` anti-selects — the best entries are fresh 10–14-min turns, not persistent mature runs; the full package (n = 6, 2 days) does not separate from its rejects (0.50 vs 0.54).

**Adopted:** keep the three simple gates that held individually (15-min flow > 0 → 0.57; clock ≤ 14:30 → 0.61/adv 0.18; weak-side ≥ 0.15) + **early invalidation** (caught 10:29, 15:09, Aug-18 10:41; cancelled no winner). Log accel/giveback/resp per fire as diagnostics; do **not** threshold them or use the Q score as rules at n = 32. Read: "enter early; once steep you're late" replaces the steep paradox — the base rule's early fire already captures ignition. Every threshold added beyond the simple three made this sample worse — Charlie's own warning applied one level deeper.

### 6.5 CIO memo — the HIRO early-turn rule, in plain terms (2026-08-21)

**What the rule does.** We watch two things at once during the trading day: the S&P 500 index, minute by minute, and SpotGamma's HIRO line — a running estimate, in dollars, of the buying or selling pressure that options trades are forcing on dealers. The rule looks for one specific moment: the market has slipped at least 3 points from its recent high, and the HIRO line, having been falling, turns up and climbs steadily for at least ten minutes at a meaningful pace (about $2 billion an hour or better) — with three boxes ticked at once: call buyers and put sellers are *both* pushing the same way (neither side flat), the shortest-dated options agree (they're at least half the flow), and the climb hasn't already given much back. When all of that lines up, we sell the put — the first leg of the delta bomb — at the next minute's opening price, and rest the buy order 5 points higher.

**The three gates and the exit.** Before pulling the trigger we require three simple things: the last 15 minutes of flow must be net positive (the pressure is still arriving, not rolling over); the clock must read 14:30 or earlier (the four late-day signals in our sample went 0-for-4); and the weaker of the two flow components must have real size — at least $150 million — so we're not acting on one-sided flow that technically qualifies. After entry there is one special exit: if within the first three minutes the HIRO line drops $300 million below where it stood when we entered, or the climb formally breaks, we scratch the trade immediately. The logic is plain — the flow *was* the reason for the trade; if the flow vanishes, the reason is gone. In the sample, that exit turned the worst trade of the week (a 12.6-point excursion against us) into a 0.8-point scratch. The standing risk rules from the main playbook still apply: one unpaired leg at a time, a 60-minute clock, buy the short back if it moves 3.5 points against us, and never the ATM anchor.

**What the evidence says, honestly.** Across the five sessions we have HIRO data for, the rule produced 18 executable trades: the second leg filled within the hour 56% of the time versus roughly 44% for entries at random minutes, the first 3 points typically arrived within 5 minutes, and the adverse tail was essentially unchanged (17% vs 19% of trades saw a 10-point run against us). Two independent reviewers — a dealer-gamma mechanic and a flows strategist — converged on the same sentence, which we've adopted: the signal shows *preliminary timing value* for pullback rebounds, and *no demonstrated protection* against the tail. In other words: HIRO helps us pick the minute; it does not make the trade safer. Safety still comes from the exit rules. An earlier, better-looking version of these numbers (65% fills, 10% tail) was withdrawn after a code review found it had been flattered by three technical errors; the figures above are the corrected, one-trade-at-a-time accounting.

**Decision and what happens next.** Trade the rule at one-lot paper on the up-trend branch only, logging every signal — including the ones the gates reject — with the diagnostic measurements (flow acceleration, giveback, price response) attached to each. No real size until two pre-registered tests pass on a bigger sample (~20 sessions, which requires the daily HIRO capture to keep running): first, compare otherwise-identical pullbacks *with* and *without* HIRO confirmation — same depth, time of day, volatility — to prove the flow adds anything beyond the pullback itself; second, strip out of the HIRO signal everything the price chart already said, and check the remainder still predicts. If either fails, the honest conclusion is that HIRO was echoing the tape, and we keep the base playbook unchanged. Everything — scripts, reviews, corrected numbers — is committed through `7c2caa8`.

### 6.6 Feynman-style memo — the same rules, from the mechanism up (2026-08-21)

Let's not start with the acronym. Picture a seesaw you can't see directly, but there's a gauge for it. When people buy call options or sell put options, the dealers on the other side end up needing to buy stock to stay balanced; buy puts or sell calls and the dealers need to sell. The HIRO line is that gauge: a running total, in dollars, of how much buying or selling the day's options trades have been pushing onto the dealers. When the line climbs, upward pressure is accumulating; when it falls, downward. Our trade needs one small thing to happen: after the market dips a few points, we sell a put, and we need the market to bounce about three points so we can buy a nearby put cheaply and finish the pair for free. So the question the rule asks is narrow: *is there a moment when the gauge tells us the bounce is being pushed on, right now?*

Here is the moment. The market has dipped at least 3 points. The gauge, which was falling, has turned and climbed for ten minutes straight at a real pace — and the climb is honest in three ways: both kinds of options flow agree (call buying *and* put selling, not one side faking it), the shortest-dated options — the ones dealers must hedge fastest — carry at least half of it, and the climb hasn't already leaked away. Then three quick checks: pressure still arriving over the last quarter hour, not after 2:30pm, and the smaller of the two flows big enough to matter. Sell the put at the next minute's open. And one promise to yourself: if within three minutes the gauge gives back $300 million or the climb breaks, get out for pennies — because the pushing was your whole reason for being there, and it stopped. That promise is not decoration; in our week of data it converted the single worst trade from a 12-point bruise into a scratch.

Now, does it work? Watch what we can measure. Eighteen trades in five days: the bounce we needed came within the hour 56% of the time, against about 44% if you'd entered at random minutes, and when it came, it usually came within five minutes — which is exactly what you'd expect if dealers really are buying against the flow *right then*. But here's the part that keeps us honest: the bad outcomes — a 10-point slide against us before any bounce — happened just as often as at random minutes. The gauge tells you *when* the push is on; it does not tell you the push will win. And a caution from the workshop: our first pass at these numbers looked much better (65% and a smaller tail), and it was wrong — we had accidentally let the rule peek at the three minutes *after* entry when deciding whether to count a trade, counted overlapping trades twice, and graded unfinished trades as failures. Finding that took an independent reading of the code. The numbers above are the repaired ones.

What would prove this real, and what have we simplified? Two tests, set down in advance. Take pairs of nearly identical dips — same depth, same hour, same choppiness — one where the gauge confirmed, one where it didn't: if they bounce equally often, the gauge added nothing. Second, subtract from the gauge everything the price chart alone already showed: if what's left predicts nothing, then the gauge was just the price wearing a different costume — "price is news." Simplifications to remember: we measured the index touching a level, not actual option fills; all five days were calm, dealer-cushioned days — on a wild day the same flow gets amplified instead of absorbed, and the rule is untested there; and eighteen trades is a story, not a statistic. The sentence to say back: *we sell the dip only when the options-pressure gauge has just turned up and everyone's pushing the same way, we quit in three minutes if the pushing stops, and so far that buys us a faster bounce — not a safer one.*

**Test protocols (the two Feynman tests, exactly as they will be run once ≥ 20 sessions exist):**

**Test 1 — matched pullbacks (does HIRO confirmation add anything beyond the dip itself?)**
1. *Universe:* every minute 09:35–14:30 with a strict 30-bar pullback ≥ 3 pts and a complete 60-min forward horizon, all sessions. One observation per pullback *episode* (first qualifying minute after each fresh 30-bar high; episodes end when a new high prints).
2. *Treatment:* episode is CONFIRMED if the §6 fire conditions hold at any minute of the episode's first 10 minutes (trough-anchored run ≥ 10 min at ≥ 2 $B/hr, C&P aligned ≥ 0.25, nextExp share ≥ 0.5, r15 > 0, weak side ≥ 0.15). UNCONFIRMED otherwise.
3. *Matching:* within-day-type strata — pullback depth bucket (3–5 / 5–8 / 8+ pts), hour bucket (10/11/12/13/14), prior-60-min realized range tercile (IM-normalised), gamma regime (SG index tercile). Each CONFIRMED episode is paired with the nearest UNCONFIRMED episode in the same stratum (no replacement); unmatched episodes dropped and counted.
4. *Outcomes:* P(+3 within 30), P(+5 within 60), time-to-+3, max adverse before +3 — measured from the episode's decision minute + 1 open in both arms.
5. *Inference:* paired differences, day-clustered bootstrap 90% CI, 2,000 draws. *Pass:* CI for Δ P(+5/60) excludes 0 **or** CI for Δ time-to-+3 excludes 0 (Charlie's speed prediction), with the sign stable in leave-one-day-out. *Fail:* both CIs cover 0 → the pullback, not the flow, was the signal.
6. *Power check before running:* need ≥ 25 matched pairs; at 5–8 confirmed episodes/week that is ≈ 4–6 more capture weeks.

**Test 2 — residualization (is HIRO just price wearing a costume?)**
1. *Frame:* every minute 10:30–14:30 with complete horizons. Response variable y = forward outcome (+5-in-60 indicator; secondarily time-to-+3).
2. *Price-only model:* logistic (y ~ price features known at t): 30-bar pullback depth, 5/15/30-min returns, 30-bar R² and sign-consistency of the price path, prior-60-min range (IM), distance to 30-bar high, hour dummies, day fixed effects. Fit walk-forward (expanding by day, predict next day).
3. *HIRO residual:* regress the fire indicator (and separately the continuous run-rate and r15) on the same price features, same walk-forward scheme; keep the residual — the part of HIRO not explained by the tape.
4. *Test:* add the residual to the price-only model. *Pass:* out-of-fold improvement in log-loss/AUC with a day-clustered CI excluding 0, and the residual's coefficient sign stable across folds. *Fail:* no improvement → "price is news"; HIRO echoes the chart and the §6 rule is a price-pullback rule with extra steps.
5. *Guard-rails:* no same-minute price fields inside the HIRO features (already true — cumulative flow only); fires per day logged so one day cannot dominate; report with and without the down-drift week.
